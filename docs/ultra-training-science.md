# Ultramarathon Training Science & Workout Library (50K, 50 Mile, 100K, 100 Mile)

> Research-backed training guide for ultra plan generation and coach context.
> Same format as the 5K/10K/half/marathon science docs: universal science first,
> then distance-specific guidance, workout libraries, periodization, and
> implementation notes for the Stride plan engine.
> Sources: Koop (*Training Essentials for Ultrarunning*), Roche & Roche (*Happy Runner* /
> SWAP coaching), Uphill Athlete (House, Johnston, Jornet — *Training for the Uphill Athlete*),
> Magness, Hoffman & Krishnan (Western States research), Costa et al. (gut training,
> exercise-associated GI symptoms), Jeukendrup (multiple transportable carbohydrates),
> Millet (neuromuscular fatigue in ultras), ACSM / IOC consensus statements on heat and hydration.

## 1. What Changes When the Race Gets Longer Than a Marathon

The marathon is limited by glycogen, threshold, and economy. Ultras keep all of those
but add **new limiters that eventually matter more than VO2max**:

| Limiter | 50K | 50 mi | 100K | 100 mi |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Aerobic engine / economy | ★★★★ | ★★★★ | ★★★ | ★★★ |
| Lactate threshold | ★★★ | ★★ | ★★ | ★ |
| Fueling & GI tolerance | ★★★ | ★★★★ | ★★★★★ | ★★★★★ |
| Muscle damage resistance (esp. downhill) | ★★★ | ★★★★ | ★★★★ | ★★★★★ |
| Pacing discipline | ★★★ | ★★★★ | ★★★★★ | ★★★★★ |
| Climbing / power hiking | course-dependent | ★★★ | ★★★ | ★★★★ |
| Heat, night, sleep deprivation | ★ | ★★ | ★★★ | ★★★★★ |
| Mental resilience / problem solving | ★★ | ★★★ | ★★★★ | ★★★★★ |

Key physiology:

- **Intensity drops as duration rises.** A 50K for a trained runner is raced roughly at
  marathon-to-steady effort (~70–80% VO2max); a 100-miler is raced at **~50–60% VO2max**
  for most of the field. Fitness at very low intensities — fat oxidation, durability, and
  economy when tired — decides results.
- **Durability is the key trait.** This is how much your economy, threshold, and
  pace hold up after hours of running. Two runners with the same fresh VO2max can
  differ hugely at hour 10. Durability is built with volume, long runs, back-to-backs,
  and downhill/eccentric loading.
- **Muscle damage, not glycogen, ends most 100s.** Eccentric loading on descents causes
  muscle fiber damage, inflammation, and central fatigue. Millet's lab work on mountain ultras
  shows large drops in maximal voluntary force (often 30–40%+) after 100-mile mountain races,
  much of it central (the brain reducing drive).
- **The gut is a trainable organ.** GI distress is among the most common reasons for
  DNFs in 100-milers. Tolerance for carbohydrate intake (60–90+ g/hr) improves with
  repeated practice (Costa et al.).
- **Pacing errors are punished late.** Going out too hard in the first 20–30% of a 100-miler
  is the classic error. Even pacing (or a slight positive split from terrain and darkness) is the
  target. In Western States finishing data, finishers tend to pace more evenly than DNFs.

## 2. Universal Principles for Ultra Training

### 2.1 Volume is king, but **time on feet** is the currency
- Switch from miles to **hours** as the main volume unit once trail/vert is involved.
  An 8-mile trail run with 2,000 ft of climbing can take as long as 12 road miles.
- Plan engine rule: on trail days, prescribe long runs **by duration** (e.g. "3h30 on
  trail, effort-based") with a distance estimate, not distance alone.

### 2.2 Intensity distribution stays polarized, maybe more so
- ~80–90% easy (Zone 1–2). Ultras do *not* mean you never run fast.
- Threshold work, hill repeats, and even some VO2max work still raise the ceiling.
  A higher ceiling makes race pace a smaller fraction of max, so it costs less.
- Koop: early in a build, prioritize **intensity** (raising the ceiling) while volume is
  moderate. Later, shift to **specific volume** (long runs, back-to-backs, vert) and
  maintain intensity at lower doses.

### 2.3 Specificity rises toward the race
- Match the course: vert per mile, terrain (technical vs. runnable), surface, heat,
  altitude, night hours, and the run/hike ratio.
- Last 8–10 weeks should simulate race demands: race-effort long runs on similar
  terrain, fueling at race rates, night running, and the gear you'll race with (vest, poles, lights).

### 2.4 Back-to-back long runs (B2B)
- Two long runs on consecutive days (e.g. Sat 20mi + Sun 12mi) build fatigue resistance
  with less damage and recovery cost than one huge run.
- Day 2 is run on pre-fatigued legs and lower glycogen, which mimics the second half of an ultra.
- Typical cap: day 1 ≈ 3–5 h, day 2 ≈ 50–75% of day 1.
- **Stride rule:** B2Bs count as a "hard day pair". The day before and the day after must be easy or rest,
  and no interval/threshold session within 48 h of day 1.

### 2.5 Longest training run cap
- Long runs give diminishing returns past ~4–5 hours and cost a lot to recover from.
  Most coaches cap single runs around:
  - 50K: 20–24 mi / 3–4 h
  - 50 mi: 26–32 mi / 4–6 h (a training 50K race is common)
  - 100K: 28–34 mi / 5–6 h, or a 50K–50 mi tune-up race
  - 100 mi: 30–35 mi / 5–7 h, or a 50 mi–100K tune-up race 6–10 weeks out, plus B2Bs
- Never run the full race distance in training.

### 2.6 Hiking is a skill, not failure
- On grades steeper than ~15–20%, power hiking is often as fast as running for less energy.
  Even elite 100-mile runners hike the steep climbs.
- Train it: uphill treadmill hikes at 15%+ incline, hiking intervals on steep climbs,
  and practice with poles if the race allows them.
- Stride rule: for races with >100 ft/mi of climbing, include 1 weekly vert session
  (hill repeats or treadmill hike) plus hiking segments inside long runs.

### 2.7 Downhill / eccentric conditioning
- Downhill running is what destroys quads in mountain 100s (Western States' early descents,
  UTMB's long descents).
- The **repeated-bout effect**: one bout of eccentric exercise protects muscle for weeks.
  Do regular controlled downhill running, starting small and building.
- Progression: 2–3 × 3–5 min controlled descents → long runs with 2,000–5,000 ft descent →
  race-simulation descents 4–8 weeks out. Last big downhill session ≥3 weeks before race.
- Strength work (step-downs, eccentric split squats, Nordic curls, single-leg RDLs) 2×/week
  in base, 1×/week maintenance in peak.

### 2.8 Fueling — the 4th discipline
- **Carbohydrate:** 60–90 g/hr is the modern target. Elite 100-mile runners often aim for 80–120 g/hr
  using glucose + fructose mixes (multiple transportable carbohydrates, Jeukendrup).
  Start training at 40–60 g/hr and build by ~10 g/hr every 1–2 weeks of long runs.
- **Sodium:** ~300–600 mg/hr typical (highly individual; heavy/salty sweaters more), plus more in heat.
- **Fluid:** drink to thirst, ~400–800 ml/hr typical. **Avoid overdrinking** (hyponatremia risk
  is real in 100-milers, especially slower finishers and in moderate temps). Losing ~2-3% of
  body weight over a 100 is normal and acceptable.
- **Real food** (potatoes, rice balls, broth, sandwiches) gets more important past ~8–10 hours as
  palate fatigue sets in.
- **Gut training:** fuel at race rates in **every** long run >90 min for the last 8–12 weeks.
  Practice the exact products and the aid-station foods on the course.
- **Caffeine:** low doses (~1–3 mg/kg total, split) late in the race and overnight. Practice it first.

### 2.9 Heat, night, and sleep
- **Heat acclimation:** 10–14 days of 60–90 min daily heat exposure (running in heat, sauna
  after runs, extra layers) gives most of the adaptation: plasma volume ↑, sweat onset earlier,
  lower core temperature and HR at the same pace. Start ~3–4 weeks out for hot races (Western States, Badwater).
- **Night running:** 100-milers take most runners through a full night. Do 2–4 long runs that
  finish in the dark with race headlamp.
- **Sleep:** bank sleep the week before. During the race, sub-24 h runners usually don't sleep.
  Back-of-pack runners in mountain 100s+ may use 10–20 min naps.

### 2.10 Recovery and injury risk
- Mileage ramp rules still apply (≤10%/week for most, ACWR 0.8–1.3).
- **Count vert as load.** A rough rule of thumb: ~100 ft (30 m) of climbing ≈ an extra 0.1 mile of effort.
  Track weekly vert alongside miles and ramp it gradually too.
- Recovery after racing (rough guide, reverse taper):
  - 50K: 1–2 weeks easy before workouts
  - 50 mi / 100K: 2–3 weeks
  - 100 mi: 3–6 weeks. Week 1 mostly rest/walk, week 2 easy short runs, weeks 3–4 gradual return.
- Red flags (stop and refer): dark/cola urine after a race (rhabdomyolysis), focal bone pain
  (stress reaction), confusion/headache with nausea (hyponatremia).

## 3. Mileage & Volume Guidelines by Distance

| Distance | Finish-focused peak | Competitive peak | Elite range (typical) | Longest single run |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50K | 35–50 mpw | 50–70 mpw | 80–110 mpw | 20–24 mi |
| 50 mi | 45–60 mpw | 60–80 mpw | 90–120 mpw | 26–32 mi |
| 100K | 50–65 mpw | 65–90 mpw | 100–130 mpw | 28–34 mi |
| 100 mi | 55–75 mpw | 70–100 mpw | 100–140+ mpw (or 15–25+ h/week) | 30–35 mi + B2B |

Prerequisites before starting a structured plan:
- **50K:** completed a marathon *or* consistently 30+ mpw for 3 months, long run ≥ 14 mi.
- **50 mi:** completed a 50K or marathon, 35+ mpw base.
- **100K:** completed a 50 mi (recommended) or strong 50K, 45+ mpw base.
- **100 mi:** completed a 50 mi or 100K (strongly recommended), 1+ year of consistent 40+ mpw.
  Many races require a qualifier anyway (Western States, UTMB, Hardrock).

Weekly vert guidelines for mountain races: peak weeks often reach **50–100% of race vert
per week** (e.g. ~10,000–18,000 ft/week for an 18,000 ft 100-miler) for competitive runners.

## 4. Periodization

Ultra plans are longer than marathon plans because the base must be bigger.

| Distance | Total build | Base | Build | Specific/Peak | Taper |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 50K | 12–16 wks | 4–6 | 4 | 3–4 | 1–2 |
| 50 mi | 16–20 wks | 6 | 5 | 4–5 | 2 |
| 100K | 18–24 wks | 6–8 | 6 | 5 | 2–3 |
| 100 mi | 20–30 wks | 8–10 | 6–8 | 6 | 2–3 |

### Phase 1 — Base (aerobic + intensity foundation)
- Build volume to ~75–80% of peak. Mostly easy running, strides 2×/week.
- 1 quality session/week: threshold (e.g. 3×10 min) **or** short hill repeats (8–10 × 60–90 s).
- Strength 2×/week. Begin small downhill exposure.
- Fueling: start practicing 40–60 g/hr on runs >90 min.

### Phase 2 — Build (raise the ceiling, add specificity)
- 2 quality sessions/week while volume rises: threshold + hills/VO2.
  Example: Tue long hill repeats (6 × 3 min), Thu tempo 30–40 min or 3 × 2 mi.
- Long run grows by ~15–30 min every 1–2 weeks. Introduce B2Bs every 2–3 weeks.
- Vert ramps up. One run/week on race-like terrain.

### Phase 3 — Specific / Peak (simulate the race)
- Quality shifts to **race-specific** work: steady-state long efforts at race effort on similar
  terrain, sustained climbs, long downhill practice, night runs, heat training if relevant.
- Biggest B2Bs and tune-up race (50K for a 50 mi/100K target, 50 mi/100K for a 100).
- Fuel at full race rate every long run. Rehearse gear, crew, and aid station routine.
- Keep 1 short sharp session (e.g. 6 × 2 min uphill or 20 min tempo) to stay sharp.

### Phase 4 — Taper
- **50K:** 7–10 days, volume −30–40%.
- **50 mi / 100K:** ~2 weeks: −25–30% then −40–50%.
- **100 mi:** 2–3 weeks: −20–30%, −40%, −50–60% race week. Keep a few short
  pickups/strides so legs stay snappy. Last long run (~3 h) about 14–18 days out.
- Taper also protects the **eccentric damage window**: no big descents in the last 10–14 days.

### Deloads
- Every 3rd–4th week for most amateurs (volume −20–30%). This matters more in ultra training,
  because tissue load (vert, B2Bs) adds up faster than cardio fitness shows.

## 5. Sample Week Structures

**50K (competitive, ~55 mpw peak)**
- Mon rest / easy 4
- Tue hills: 2 mi WU, 8 × 90 s uphill hard, jog down, 2 mi CD
- Wed easy 7 + strides
- Thu tempo: 2 mi WU, 30 min steady-tempo, 1 mi CD
- Fri easy 5 / rest
- Sat long 18–22 mi on trail with race-effort middle 6–8 mi
- Sun easy 8–10 (B2B lite)

**50 mi / 100K (~70 mpw peak)**
- Mon rest
- Tue threshold: 4 × 10 min at threshold, 2 min jog
- Wed easy 8 + strides
- Thu vert session: 60–75 min with 2,000–3,000 ft climbing, or treadmill hike 15% × 45 min
- Fri easy 6
- Sat long 4–5 h trail, fuel at race rate
- Sun B2B 2–2.5 h easy on tired legs

**100 mi (~80–90 mpw peak, mountain)**
- Mon rest / 30 min walk
- Tue easy 8 + strides (or double: AM 6, PM 4)
- Wed hill session: 6 × 4 min uphill at 10K–half effort + long controlled descent
- Thu easy 10
- Fri easy 6 + mobility
- Sat long 5–6 h race-terrain simulation, includes night finish 1×/month
- Sun B2B 3 h easy/steady, hiking climbs

## 6. Workout Library

### Intensity / ceiling sessions (base → build)
1. **Threshold cruise intervals:** 3–5 × 8–12 min at threshold (~1 h race effort), 2 min jog.
2. **Continuous tempo:** 20–40 min at threshold/steady.
3. **Short hill repeats:** 8–12 × 45–90 s at ~5K effort on 6–10% grade, jog down. Builds power and economy with low impact.
4. **Long hill repeats:** 4–8 × 3–6 min at 10K–half effort uphill. VO2max work disguised as climbing.
5. **Fartlek on trail:** 45–60 min with 10–15 × 1–2 min surges at ~10K effort.
6. **Strides:** 6 × 20 s fast-relaxed, 2×/week, all phases.

### Specific endurance sessions (build → peak)
7. **Race-effort long run:** e.g. 4 h with the middle 2 h at goal race effort on similar terrain.
8. **Back-to-back weekend:** Sat 4–5 h / Sun 2–3 h. Day 2 starts on tired legs. Keep it easy.
9. **Vert session:** 2,000–5,000 ft in 1.5–3 h. Run the runnable parts, power hike the steep ones.
10. **Treadmill hike intervals:** 4–6 × 8 min at 15–20% incline, brisk, 2 min easy. For flatlanders.
11. **Downhill conditioning:** 4–6 × 3–5 min controlled descents at 6–10% grade, quick cadence, light feet.
12. **Steady-state progression:** 90–120 min starting easy, last 30–40 min at marathon-to-50K effort.
13. **Night run:** long run starting late afternoon, last 1.5–2 h in the dark with race headlamp.
14. **Fueling rehearsal run:** 3 h+ at race effort using only race products at target g/hr, logging GI symptoms.
15. **Tune-up race:** 50K or 50 mi raced at "training effort" (controlled), 6–10 weeks out.

### Road / flat ultra variants (100K road, Tunnel Hill-style 100s, track 24 h)
- Treat like "marathon plus": higher mileage, long runs with long blocks at goal pace
  (e.g. 3 × 5 mi at 100K pace inside a 24 mi run), frequent doubles, and lots of practice
  holding even splits. Road 100K pace for a 2:58 marathoner is usually ~8:00–8:45/mi.

## 7. How the Pros Train

These summaries come from interviews, published training logs, and coach write-ups.
Details change year to year. Use them to show **patterns**, not as plans to copy.

- **Jim Walmsley** (Western States course record holder, multiple-time WSER champion,
  UTMB champion 2023): very high volume for his race blocks, often **100–140+ mpw** with large
  vert, based in Flagstaff (altitude). He keeps real speed (track/road background,
  sub-2:15 marathon), runs frequent doubles, and does long runs on race-like terrain. Big lesson:
  **a high aerobic ceiling + volume + course specificity.**
- **Courtney Dauwalter** (won Western States, Hardrock, and UTMB in the same summer, 2023):
  famously loose structure. No formal plan, runs by feel, high volume (~100+ mpw in builds),
  very little structured speed work, eats whatever works, and is known for mental toughness ("the pain cave").
  Lesson: **consistency and enjoying the process count for a lot. Rigid structure is optional
  for some athletes, and durability and mindset are trainable.**
- **Kilian Jornet** (multiple UTMB, Hardrock, and Western States wins): measures training in **hours
  and vertical**, not miles. Very large annual vert, lots of ski-mountaineering in winter
  (huge aerobic volume with low impact), and strength/mountain skills. Co-authored
  *Training for the Uphill Athlete*. Lesson: **for mountain races, vert and time are the units, and cross-training
  can carry aerobic volume.**
- **Camille Herron** (former 100 mi and 24 h world-record holder on road/track): a road-marathon
  background with high mileage and frequent doubles, and treats ultras like long marathons with
  aggressive fueling. Lesson: **for flat/road ultras, marathon-style training scaled up works.**
- **Aleksandr Sorokin** (100 km and 100 mile world-record holder): road/track focus, high
  mileage with lots of steady running near goal pace and long continuous efforts. Lesson: **on
  flat courses, goal-pace volume is the specific stimulus.**
- **Zach Bitter** (former 100 mi track record holder): high mileage, a lot of training on
  flat terrain and treadmill matched to track specificity. He's known for metabolic efficiency (low-carb
  daily diet with carbs around key sessions). Fueling approaches like his are individual. The general
  evidence still favors high carbohydrate intake during racing.

**Common threads across elites:**
1. Massive *consistent* aerobic volume, built over many years, not one cycle.
2. Fast running isn't abandoned. Most can run fast at shorter distances.
3. Specific preparation for terrain (vert, descents, heat, altitude).
4. Fueling treated as training, with high carb rates practiced often.
5. Strategic racing: few goal ultras per year (2–4), with full recovery between.

## 8. Pacing & Race Execution

- **Start slower than feels right.** In the first 25% of a 100, HR should sit in low Zone 2. If
  you're breathing hard on early climbs, hike.
- **Effort/HR over pace on trails.** Use pace only for flat road/track ultras.
- **Rough race pace from marathon fitness (flat-ish courses):**
  - 50K ≈ marathon pace + 30–60 s/mi
  - 50 mi ≈ marathon pace + 1:30–2:30/mi
  - 100K ≈ marathon pace + 2:00–3:00/mi
  - 100 mi ≈ marathon pace + 3:30–5:00+/mi (including aid stations and hiking)
  Mountain courses: use course history (compare your time to runners with similar marathon/50K
  times) rather than formulas.
- **Aid stations:** have a plan. "In and out" by default, with a checklist for each crew stop.
- **Problem-solving:** the race is a series of problems (nausea, blisters, lows). Have a
  plan for each one: slow down, cool down, eat, fix feet early.

## 9. Stride Implementation Notes (for the plan engine)

When ultra distances are enabled (Phase 2+ in CLAUDE.md), the engine should:

1. **Goal inputs:** distance (50k/50mi/100k/100mi), race date, total vert (ft), terrain
   (road/runnable trail/mountain), expected heat, and whether poles are allowed.
2. **Volume unit:** switch to hours for trail/mountain goals. Track weekly vert as a second
   load metric with its own ramp limit (~10–15%/week) and include it in ACWR.
3. **Weekly template:** Mon rest, Tue quality (threshold/hills), Wed easy, Thu vert/second quality,
   Fri easy/rest, Sat long, Sun B2B (every 2–3 weeks in build/peak).
4. **Back-to-backs** count as one hard block. Enforce easy days around them.
5. **Long run prescription** by duration + effort + fueling target, e.g.
   "4h on trail, easy-to-steady effort, hike >15% grades, 70 g carbs/hr + 500 mg sodium/hr".
6. **Fueling progression:** start at 40–60 g/hr, increase 10 g/hr every 1–2 long runs to
   the target (60–90 g/hr). Ask about GI symptoms in post-run feedback for long runs.
7. **Taper** auto-length from distance (50K 7–10 d, 50 mi/100K 14 d, 100 mi 14–21 d).
8. **Post-race recovery** blocks are automatic and conservative (see §2.10).
9. **Readiness gates:** don't allow a 100 mi goal without a prior 50 mi/100K or a long
   enough runway (≥ 24 weeks with ≥ 40 mpw base). Suggest an intermediate goal instead,
   following the "fail safe" principle.
10. **Coach feedback** should look at: HR drift on long runs (aerobic durability),
    pace at the same HR over time, fueling notes, and hike/run discipline on climbs.

### Jake-specific note
Jake (2:58 marathon, 50–70 mpw history, future 100-miler) fits the
"competitive 50K → 50 mi → 100K → 100 mi" ladder over 18–30 months:
1. 50K trail at ~60 mpw (12–16 wk build) after the sub-17 5K block,
2. 50 mi with B2Bs and vert focus at 65–75 mpw,
3. 100K or 100 mi qualifier race at 75–90 mpw,
with full recovery and a speed block between each. Keep 5K/10K-type speed
work alive at low doses all the way through. It's what keeps his ceiling high.
