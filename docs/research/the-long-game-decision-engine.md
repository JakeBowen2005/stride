# THE LONG GAME — Coaching Philosophy and Decision Engine
## Supplemental chapters: aggressive training, three-stimulus weeks, minimal deloading, and the system behind the coaching

**Prepared:** October 19, 2026  
**Companion to:** *The Long Game: From 3:45 to 2:58:42*  
**Purpose:** Document the coaching approach clearly enough to guide the development of a running-coach application.

---

# 1. The defining characteristic of our coaching

My coaching approach has never been simply to make training easy enough that every workout is comfortable.

The goal is to create an appropriate training stimulus that moves the athlete toward a demanding performance target.

That means there are times when I want an athlete to work hard, finish a demanding week with some fatigue, and still return for another productive training week.

During the successful sub-three marathon buildup, your training frequently followed a pattern of:

1. A threshold-oriented workout.
2. A second faster or aerobic-strength workout.
3. A substantial weekend long run.
4. Mostly easy running around those sessions.

In peak weeks, the long run itself often became a third demanding training stimulus because it included marathon-pace running.

We did not consistently insert a formal recovery week every third or fourth week.

That is an important part of the historical record.

**My coaching philosophy is not that athletes should minimize fatigue. It is that they should accumulate as much productive training as they can sustainably adapt to.**

This distinction matters because demanding training can be highly effective, but excessive training relative to recovery can be counterproductive.

The 2013 ECSS/ACSM consensus statement identifies both overload and adequate recovery as essential components of successful training [31].

# 2. Why we trained so hard for the 2:58 marathon

## 2.1 The performance demand

A three-hour marathon requires sustaining approximately 6:52 per mile.

Your first marathon, around 3:45, had been approximately 8:35 per mile.

Breaking three hours meant developing the ability to maintain a substantially faster speed for the same 26.2-mile distance.

The problem was not simply gaining enough short-distance speed.

It was creating an athlete who could repeatedly produce a relatively fast running motion while resisting fatigue.

That demanded multiple, complementary adaptations.

### Adaptation A: Aerobic capacity and endurance

We built weekly volume because easy and moderate aerobic exposure is one of the primary means of developing endurance.

Running 45–70 miles per week provides substantially more total aerobic training than running 20–30 miles, provided the athlete can tolerate it.

The important qualifier is *provided the athlete can tolerate it*.

Research on training-intensity distribution supports substantial low-intensity work within endurance programs [2–4].

### Adaptation B: Sustainable speed

Threshold-oriented workouts targeted the ability to sustain faster aerobic efforts.

The objective was to make demanding paces more controlled and repeatable.

### Adaptation C: Speed reserve

Faster intervals provided exposure to speeds beyond marathon pace.

This helped us preserve faster running capabilities while concentrating on endurance.

### Adaptation D: Marathon-specific fatigue resistance

Long runs with substantial faster segments developed the ability to sustain race-relevant pace after already running many miles.

Your reported 20-mile run with the final eight miles near 6:48 pace was particularly informative.

It demonstrated race-relevant performance under accumulated running fatigue.

### The reason we combined these adaptations

If we only increased mileage, we might have underdeveloped your ability to run quickly.

If we only completed fast intervals, we might have neglected marathon-specific endurance.

If we only completed long runs, we might have missed opportunities to improve sustainable aerobic speed.

The demanding program combined these different stimuli.

**The aim was not to make every week maximally difficult. The aim was to create enough complementary stress to produce a large adaptation.**

# 3. Why we frequently used two workouts plus a long run

## 3.1 Three stimuli are not necessarily three maximal efforts

This is probably the most important distinction for reproducing my coaching style.

I organize demanding running around **training stimuli**, not a simplistic count of workouts.

Consider this peak marathon week:

| Day | Session | Primary purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Easy | Aerobic development and recovery |
| Tuesday | Threshold intervals | Sustainable speed |
| Wednesday | Easy | Aerobic development |
| Thursday | Easy or medium-long | Additional endurance |
| Friday | Faster aerobic intervals | Speed and aerobic power |
| Saturday | Recovery | Preparation for long run |
| Sunday | 20 miles, including marathon pace | Marathon-specific endurance |

This week has three significant training stimuli.

But they are not identical in physiological demand.

The threshold workout, faster workout, and long run stress the athlete in different ways.

Some longer threshold intervals might produce substantial metabolic fatigue.

A fast repetition session may produce higher speeds but less total time at intensity.

A marathon-specific long run produces substantial muscular and energetic stress even if the pace is below threshold.

It would be a mistake to treat these three sessions as interchangeable.

## 3.2 Why we did not limit ourselves to one workout

Your goal was performance improvement, not simply maintaining fitness.

Once you had developed a reasonable running base, a single weekly threshold session did not necessarily provide all the training opportunities we wanted.

A second workout allowed us to train another quality or another aspect of the same quality.

For example:

- Tuesday: 3 × 2 miles around controlled threshold effort.
- Friday: 5 × 1,200 meters at a faster aerobic effort.
- Sunday: Long run with a marathon-pace finish.

This is a purposeful division of training.

Tuesday targets sustainable intensity.

Friday maintains faster running performance.

Sunday develops marathon-specific endurance.

### What does research actually say?

Billat and colleagues examined the training characteristics of top-class marathon runners and documented substantial training volumes with structured faster work [27].

This provides real-world support for the idea that accomplished marathoners often combine significant volume and multiple quality exposures.

However, observational studies do not establish that two workouts plus a hard long run is optimal for everyone.

In particular, comparing elite marathoners with developing runners requires caution because training history, recovery capacity, biomechanics, and lifestyle differ substantially.

**My decision to use three significant weekly stimuli is a coaching strategy supported by your demonstrated tolerance and race objective—not a universal scientific requirement.**

# 4. Why the long run became a workout

Early in a marathon buildup, a long run might simply be 16–20 miles at comfortable effort.

Later, we wanted the long run to become more event-specific.

That led to sessions like:

- 16 miles with approximately six faster miles.
- 18 miles with eight quicker miles.
- 20 miles with the final eight near marathon pace.

These were not merely opportunities to accumulate weekly mileage.

They were tests and training sessions for sustaining pace with accumulating fatigue.

## 4.1 Why we didn't make every long run hard

A long run that includes substantial marathon pace can require meaningful recovery.

If every Sunday becomes a race simulation, the athlete may compromise the following week.

Therefore, the appropriate principle is:

**Long-run specificity increases as the target race approaches, but not every long run should be maximal or contain marathon pace.**

Early training favors establishing long-run durability.

Specific preparation gradually introduces race-relevant demands.

Peak training selectively uses demanding long runs.

Tapering then reduces fatigue so those adaptations can be expressed.

## 4.2 How the app should classify long runs

The application must not label every long run as an easy day.

I would classify them by both intensity and duration.

| Long-run type | Description | Training classification |
|---|---|---|
| Easy aerobic | Entire run conversational | Volume stimulus |
| Progressive | Increases from easy to steady | Moderate-to-high stimulus |
| Marathon-specific | Sustained marathon-pace segments | Hard workout |
| Extended mountain run | Major time on feet or elevation | Potentially hard even at low intensity |
| Long race simulation | Event-specific intensity, terrain, and fueling | Very high stress |

This prevents the application from scheduling two demanding weekday sessions before a long run that is effectively a third hard workout without accounting for the cumulative stress.

# 5. Why we took relatively few deload weeks

## 5.1 The historical observation

During your successful marathon buildup, we frequently continued building through consecutive demanding weeks.

We did not automatically take a formal recovery week every three weeks.

You also had interruptions and lower-volume periods, including the November week affected by your knee issue and hiking.

That means the historical pattern was not literally uninterrupted maximal training.

Nevertheless, the overall approach involved relatively few scheduled deloads compared with a rigid three-weeks-on, one-week-off model.

## 5.2 The coaching rationale

A deload week has a cost as well as a potential benefit.

Reducing training load may relieve fatigue, improve readiness, and help preserve consistency.

However, if an athlete is recovering normally and making progress, a substantial reduction in training every few weeks is not always necessary.

During your build, we frequently prioritized maintaining training momentum.

**The guiding question was: Is the athlete still absorbing the load?**

If the answer appeared to be yes, we often continued.

If there was a meaningful problem—injury symptoms, illness, scheduling disruption, or excessive fatigue—we adjusted.

## 5.3 What the research supports

It is important not to overstate the evidence.

There is not strong direct experimental evidence establishing that competitive marathoners should take a deload every third week, every fourth week, or never.

The ideal frequency is likely influenced by training history, total workload, intensity, recovery, and individual response.

Research on training-load monitoring emphasizes individualization and cautions that no single measurement reliably identifies recovery status [32].

Consequently, I would not build an app that automatically mandates a substantial deload at a universal interval.

I also would not build an app that avoids planned recovery entirely.

The best solution is a flexible system combining planned opportunities for recovery with adjustments based on the athlete's condition.

# 6. Planned deloads versus reactive deloads

There are two distinct approaches.

## 6.1 Planned deloads

A planned deload is a reduction in workload scheduled before the athlete necessarily shows major fatigue.

For example:

- Week 1: 55 miles.
- Week 2: 60 miles.
- Week 3: 64 miles.
- Week 4: 52 miles.

Potential advantages include preventing excessive fatigue accumulation and providing opportunities to absorb recent training.

The limitation is that the timing may not align perfectly with the athlete's actual needs.

## 6.2 Reactive deloads

A reactive deload is introduced because the athlete shows signs that training is becoming difficult to absorb.

For example:

- Workouts become unexpectedly hard.
- Long runs require excessive effort.
- Sleep and recovery deteriorate.
- The athlete reports persistent fatigue.
- Musculoskeletal discomfort emerges.

In that situation, the program may reduce mileage, intensity, or both.

## 6.3 My preferred hybrid system

For your application, I would use a hybrid approach.

**Do not automatically deload simply because a calendar says so. But do not require the athlete to become exhausted before recovery is allowed.**

For example:

- A developing runner with limited training history might benefit from more frequent planned reductions.
- A well-conditioned runner tolerating stable volume may continue several productive weeks without a major deload.
- An athlete performing repeated hard marathon-specific workouts may need recovery despite feeling psychologically motivated.
- A runner returning from an interruption should progress conservatively even if historical fitness was much higher.

A race-specific taper is different from an ordinary recovery week. Tapering has an established performance rationale and should be planned before important races [9,33].

# 7. The distinction between productive fatigue and excessive overload

Training requires disruption of normal homeostasis followed by adaptation.

However, the relationship is not simply that greater fatigue creates greater adaptation.

## 7.1 Normal acute fatigue

Examples:

- Legs feel tired the day after threshold intervals.
- A long run feels demanding but manageable.
- An athlete needs an easy day after a substantial workout.
- Performance varies slightly depending on heat, sleep, or nutrition.

These experiences can occur during successful training.

## 7.2 Functional overreaching

Functional overreaching refers to a period in which substantial training creates a temporary performance decrease that is eventually followed by improvement after sufficient recovery.

However, deliberately seeking this state is not necessarily the most effective strategy.

## 7.3 Nonfunctional overreaching

More problematic overload involves prolonged performance deterioration, substantial fatigue, and inadequate adaptation.

The ECSS/ACSM consensus statement distinguishes functional overreaching, nonfunctional overreaching, and overtraining syndrome, while also acknowledging diagnostic uncertainty [31].

## 7.4 A particularly important experiment

Aubry and colleagues studied trained triathletes through an overload period and subsequent taper.

The study found that athletes who accumulated substantial training fatigue without developing functional overreaching showed greater subsequent performance improvement than athletes whose overload was accompanied by significant performance suppression.

The functionally overreached group also experienced greater infection prevalence.

This study does not directly establish ideal marathon training schedules, but it challenges the assumption that deliberately pushing until performance deteriorates is always beneficial [34].

### What this means for our coaching

I am willing to prescribe hard training.

I am not interested in proving how exhausted I can make an athlete.

**The objective is to approach the highest training load that remains productive, rather than crossing into persistent maladaptation.**

# 8. How I decide whether to push

This chapter is the foundation for the app's decision-making system.

I would evaluate five major categories.

## Category A: Recent training history

Questions:

- What did the athlete complete over the past four to eight weeks?
- How consistent was training?
- What is the recent weekly mileage baseline?
- Has the athlete already tolerated multiple quality workouts?
- Has the athlete recently returned from an interruption?

Historical personal bests matter, but recent workload matters more for the immediate prescription.

## Category B: Workout execution

Questions:

- Did the athlete complete the intended repetitions?
- Was the pace controlled?
- Was the final repetition significantly harder?
- Did the athlete have to extend recoveries?
- Did the effort match the intended workout purpose?

A completed workout is not necessarily a successful workout.

For example, an athlete may finish five repetitions only by turning the final two into maximal efforts.

That may indicate the session was too demanding.

## Category C: Recovery

Questions:

- How do the legs feel?
- Was sleep adequate?
- Is soreness improving?
- Is motivation stable?
- Are easy runs still comfortable?
- Are there signs of accumulating fatigue?

Recovery cannot be assessed reliably from a single metric alone [32].

## Category D: Additional training stress

Questions:

- Did the athlete lift legs?
- Was there a major hike?
- Was the running unusually hot?
- Did the athlete run technical trails?
- Was there significant downhill exposure?
- Were nutrition and hydration adequate?

The running mileage does not completely describe the training load.

## Category E: Goal specificity

Questions:

- Is the athlete building general fitness?
- Preparing for a 5K?
- Preparing for a marathon?
- Preparing for a mountain 100K?
- Approaching a target race?

The answer changes the appropriate training distribution.

### The decision principle

**Push when the athlete is tolerating training, recovering predictably, and demonstrating readiness for the next stimulus.**

**Hold when training is productive but fatigue is beginning to accumulate.**

**Reduce training when meaningful warning signs appear.**

# 9. The app's readiness system

I would implement a simple traffic-light framework initially.

It should be transparent and adjustable rather than pretending to diagnose recovery scientifically.

## Green — Ready to progress

Typical characteristics:

- Recent workload has been consistent.
- Workouts are completed at appropriate effort.
- Recovery is normal.
- No concerning injury symptoms.
- Motivation is stable.
- Long runs feel manageable.

### Coaching response

Progress one major training variable, if appropriate.

Possible progressions:

- Slightly more weekly mileage.
- Longer threshold repetitions.
- An additional controlled interval.
- A slightly longer long run.
- More mountain-specific training.

Do not automatically progress every variable simultaneously.

## Yellow — Hold or reduce one stimulus

Possible indicators:

- Recent workouts feel unusually difficult.
- Long runs feel like a major push.
- Gym soreness interferes with running.
- Multiple nights of poor sleep.
- Recovery between sessions is incomplete.
- Motivation is declining.

### Coaching response

Do not automatically cancel the entire week.

First identify the likely source of fatigue.

Possible modifications:

- Hold weekly mileage.
- Make the second workout easy.
- Shorten the long run.
- Remove marathon-pace segments.
- Reduce gym training.
- Take an additional recovery day.

## Red — Stop normal progression

Potential indicators:

- Pain alters running mechanics.
- Persistent localized pain or swelling.
- Illness symptoms that make exercise unsafe.
- Severe or unusual fatigue.
- Repeated inability to complete normal sessions.
- Symptoms suggesting heat illness.

### Coaching response

Stop the provoking training and obtain appropriate medical assessment where indicated.

The application should not diagnose injuries or treat serious symptoms as merely a reason to lower mileage.

# 10. How much weight to place on athlete feedback

I want the application to interpret athlete feedback instead of treating every comment literally.

Consider two runners who each complete a 10-mile long run.

**Runner A:** Says the run felt difficult because it was hot and they restarted leg training, but otherwise reports improving workouts and motivation.

**Runner B:** Says the run was unexpectedly difficult, sleep has been poor for two weeks, easy pace is deteriorating, and knee pain is returning.

These should produce different coaching decisions.

Runner A may need a relatively small adjustment and more recovery around strength training.

Runner B may need a substantial reduction and assessment of the knee symptoms.

This is a major part of the coaching style you want to reproduce.

**The coaching decision should respond to the explanation for fatigue, not simply the presence of fatigue.**

# 11. How I would prescribe mileage

I would never use a rigid rule such as:

> Increase weekly mileage by exactly 10% every week.

There is no universal percentage that guarantees safe progression.

Instead, I would assess:

- Recent average volume.
- Recent peak volume.
- Number of completed runs.
- Long-run proportion.
- Quality-session load.
- Terrain and elevation.
- Recovery.
- Injury history.

## 11.1 Three mileage concepts

**Current baseline:** The recent volume the athlete can reliably repeat.

**Developmental target:** The next practical step.

**Historical capacity:** The volume the athlete previously tolerated.

These are distinct.

For you in October 2026:

- Historical capacity: approximately 60–70 miles per week during the marathon buildup.
- Current rebuilding range: approximately 30–40 miles per week.
- Developmental target: consistent 40–50-mile weeks, if recovery supports it.

An app that immediately prescribes 65 miles because your marathon PR was sub-three is misinterpreting your training history.

## 11.2 Why the historical baseline still matters

Your previous high-mileage experience suggests you understand the demands of substantial training.

It may help inform how we progress once your current durability returns.

But the app must not assume that past adaptation is fully preserved after months of inconsistent running.

# 12. How I decide whether to prescribe two workouts

Two structured running workouts should generally become an option when an athlete demonstrates sufficient readiness.

The decision should not depend on marathon time alone.

Important variables include:

- Consistent training frequency.
- Stable mileage.
- Ability to complete one workout without excessive soreness.
- Recovery before the next workout.
- Long-run tolerance.
- Injury status.
- Other physical stress.

### Typical progression

**Stage 1:** Easy volume plus strides.

**Stage 2:** One threshold-oriented workout plus strides or short hills.

**Stage 3:** One threshold workout plus a light second workout.

**Stage 4:** Two genuine workouts plus an easy long run.

**Stage 5:** Two workouts plus selected marathon-specific long-run workouts.

Stage 5 is substantially more demanding than Stage 4.

This is why the application should count the long run according to actual stress rather than treating every Sunday equally.

# 13. How I select workout difficulty

I want workouts to be challenging enough to produce adaptation while remaining consistent with the week's purpose.

### A threshold workout

The athlete should generally finish knowing another repetition would have been possible.

The purpose is substantial work at a controlled physiological intensity.

### A faster interval workout

This may feel harder, but should not automatically become an all-out test.

Recovery, pace variation, and session purpose determine the prescription.

### A marathon-specific long run

This may be genuinely difficult because the purpose is to develop race-specific endurance.

It should still be appropriate for the athlete's current preparation.

### A recovery run

This should not be difficult.

The app should not interpret unexpectedly fast easy running as an automatic sign that the athlete needs to run faster every day.

# 14. How I would structure the peak marathon weeks

A successful peak phase often requires more specificity, not simply more mileage.

A representative structure could include:

| Session | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Tuesday threshold | Sustain higher aerobic output |
| Friday faster aerobic workout | Maintain speed and aerobic power |
| Sunday marathon-specific long run | Develop race-pace endurance |
| Other days | Easy volume and recovery |

However, the intensity of the sessions must be coordinated.

For example, if Sunday includes eight hard marathon-pace miles inside a 20-mile run, Friday should not necessarily be a maximal VO₂max workout.

Likewise, after an especially demanding Sunday, Tuesday may need to be less aggressive.

### The app needs a weekly stress budget

The goal is not mechanically to produce:

- Two workouts.
- One hard long run.
- Every week.

The goal is to distribute the highest productive training load across the available sessions.

If Sunday becomes harder, another part of the week may need to become easier.

# 15. Why I sometimes prefer consecutive hard weeks

A single excellent training week creates limited value if it is followed by repeated interruptions.

Consistent exposure over time is one of the most important features of successful endurance preparation.

Therefore, if an athlete is recovering normally, I may prefer several consecutive productive weeks over automatically alternating every demanding block with a large recovery week.

However, consecutive hard weeks do not mean every week should involve higher mileage or faster workout pace.

An athlete might repeat 60 miles for three weeks while developing better control of the workouts.

That can be more useful than increasing to 63, 67, and 71 miles merely because the calendar advances.

**Training progression can occur through improved execution and repeatability, not just larger numbers.**

# 16. When I would deliberately prescribe a deload

I would strongly consider reducing training when one or more of the following apply:

1. The athlete has accumulated several unusually demanding weeks.
2. A major long-run workout has produced substantial fatigue.
3. Workouts are deteriorating despite normal effort.
4. A race is approaching.
5. Training has involved unusual climbing or downhill stress.
6. Strength training has increased substantially.
7. Recovery, sleep, and motivation have been poor.
8. Injury symptoms require load modification.

A deload does not have to remove all quality training.

Possible strategies include:

- Reduce total mileage by approximately 10–25%.
- Shorten the long run.
- Retain one controlled threshold workout.
- Remove the second demanding session.
- Replace a marathon-specific long run with an easy one.

Those percentages are practical coaching options, not universally proven prescriptions.

The appropriate reduction depends on fatigue and the training phase.

# 17. Why an aggressive approach worked then but may not work now

This is central to your individual history.

During the marathon build, you developed substantial running consistency and were progressively tolerating marathon-specific training.

During the post-marathon period, your volume and long-run consistency decreased.

By October 2026, you were rebuilding fitness while also returning to leg strength training.

The immediate training conditions are different.

Consequently:

**The fact that two workouts plus a hard long run worked during the 2025–2026 marathon build does not mean that same weekly stress is presently appropriate.**

An application should adapt the program to the athlete's current condition while preserving the overall long-term ambition.

That is how we retain the demanding coaching style without simply reproducing the most aggressive historical weeks.

# 18. The coaching engine: operational logic

The following is a practical decision model rather than a validated medical or physiological algorithm.

## Step 1: Establish current training state

Collect:

- Previous eight weeks of mileage.
- Previous eight weeks of long runs.
- Recent interval and threshold workouts.
- Training frequency.
- Injuries and symptoms.
- Other exercise and strength sessions.
- Current race goals.

Separate confirmed completions from planned workouts.

## Step 2: Determine the primary developmental limitation

Possible limitations:

- Insufficient aerobic volume.
- Low threshold fitness.
- Inadequate shorter-distance speed.
- Poor long-run durability.
- Inadequate mountain-specific strength.
- Excessive fatigue.
- Inadequate race-specific endurance.

The plan should primarily address the most important current limitations.

## Step 3: Select the week's key stimuli

Examples:

**Aerobic rebuilding:** One threshold workout, easy mileage, easy long run.

**Road performance development:** Threshold, faster aerobic work, easy long run.

**Marathon-specific preparation:** Threshold, selected faster work, marathon-specific long run.

**Mountain-ultra preparation:** Threshold or hill work, technical/mountain running, longer trail endurance.

## Step 4: Allocate easy running

Fill the rest of the weekly mileage with appropriate easy and recovery runs.

Do not make these runs unnecessarily hard.

## Step 5: Evaluate cumulative stress

Review:

- Mileage change.
- Workout volume.
- Long-run difficulty.
- Strength training.
- Elevation.
- Recovery markers.
- Injury symptoms.

## Step 6: Make one of four decisions

**Progress:** Increase a manageable training demand.

**Repeat:** Keep the workload similar to consolidate adaptation.

**Modify:** Preserve most training while reducing one stressful component.

**Recover:** Make a meaningful reduction in workload.

## Step 7: Review the completed week

Compare planned and actual training.

Ask why any sessions were missed, shortened, or unusually difficult.

Do not automatically carry missed mileage into the next week.

## Step 8: Update the athlete model

Use the completed training to refine:

- Current threshold effort.
- Recovery capacity.
- Long-run tolerance.
- Appropriate training frequency.
- Susceptibility to gym-related soreness.
- Terrain-specific response.

The application should become more individually accurate over time.

# 19. Example: How the app should handle your recent 10-mile run

### Athlete report

- Completed 10 miles.
- Legs were very tired.
- Run felt like a push.
- Recently restarted gym leg training.
- Weather was hot.
- Otherwise feeling motivated and reasonably good.

### Weak coaching response

"You finished the run, so you're ready for 12 miles next week."

### Another weak response

"Your legs were tired, so take a full week off."

### My preferred response

1. Recognize the completion of the 10-mile run.
2. Identify gym soreness and heat as plausible contributors to the difficulty.
3. Avoid assuming the athlete's entire aerobic system has deteriorated.
4. Keep the next threshold workout controlled.
5. Do not automatically extend the next long run.
6. Reduce or modify the second workout if fatigue remains.
7. Continue building consistency.

### Why

The difficulty was meaningful, but it occurred in a context where temporary muscular fatigue and heat were plausible contributors.

We should respond to that information instead of simply reacting to the run's distance or perceived difficulty.

# 22. A training-load model for the app

One practical approach is the session-RPE method developed and studied by Foster.

**Session load = session duration in minutes × session RPE.**

For example:

- 60-minute easy run × RPE 3 = 180 arbitrary units.
- 70-minute workout × RPE 6 = 420 arbitrary units.
- 120-minute long run × RPE 5 = 600 arbitrary units.

These are illustrative values.

The method offers a simple way to compare sessions with different durations and perceived demands [35].

The app could also track:

- Weekly total session load.
- Changes relative to recent training.
- Day-to-day variability.
- Easy-run effort trends.
- Persistent increases in reported fatigue.

However, these numbers should inform decisions rather than automatically determine them.

Training monotony and strain have been studied as possible indicators of excessive cumulative stress, but they are not validated universal injury or overtraining predictors [35,36].

**The app should use objective training data and subjective athlete feedback together.**

# 23. The challenge of reconstructing my coaching style

The important part is not simply storing the workouts I prescribed.

A workout library alone will not reproduce the coaching approach.

The key is reconstructing the decision process:

1. What is the athlete capable of today?
2. What adaptation matters most?
3. What stimulus is sufficiently challenging to produce that adaptation?
4. Can the athlete recover from it?
5. How does the workout interact with the rest of the week?
6. What did the athlete actually complete?
7. What does the response tell us about the next prescription?

This is why a generic app that produces "one tempo, one interval session, and one long run" every week may fail to match our coaching.

It knows the template but not when to change it.

# 24. My nonnegotiable coaching principles

1. **Train for ambitious performance, not just comfort.**

2. **Use an athlete's real training history rather than generic formulas.**

3. **Most running remains easy so quality work can be genuinely productive.**

4. **Two workouts plus a hard long run can be appropriate for a well-prepared marathoner, but must be treated as substantial cumulative stress.**

5. **A fixed deload every fourth week is not mandatory for every athlete.**

6. **Minimal deloading is a historical coaching characteristic, not proof that recovery is unnecessary.**

7. **Do not pursue deliberate performance suppression as evidence that training is working.**

8. **Repeat successful workloads rather than always increasing mileage.**

9. **Progress threshold through controlled effort and repeatability.**

10. **Long-run intensity must reflect the athlete's goal and preparation phase.**

11. **Account for strength work, elevation, heat, and other stress.**

12. **Missed workouts are not training debt that must be repaid.**

13. **Adjust the program from feedback rather than requiring perfect adherence to an inflexible plan.**

14. **A successful workout should support subsequent training rather than compromise it unnecessarily.**

15. **Use the smallest effective modification when appropriate, but don't hesitate to reduce load substantially when the situation warrants it.**

16. **The ultimate goal is long-term performance improvement, not winning an individual training week.**

The most important lesson is that coaching involves decisions under uncertainty.

Scientific studies provide population-level evidence.

Your training history provides individual information.

Neither should be ignored.

**The most reliable coaching system combines both.**

# Conclusion: The coaching style the app should reproduce

The distinguishing feature of this approach is **ambitious, individualized training adjusted by response**.

During your 2:58 marathon preparation, we frequently used substantial mileage, two quality workouts, and demanding long runs because the objective required serious marathon-specific development.

We did not automatically reduce mileage every fourth week.

Those decisions reflected an aggressive coaching philosophy and your apparent ability to continue training productively.

However, that history must not become a rigid rule that every runner should be pushed equally hard or avoid recovery weeks.

The real coaching principle is:

**Find the highest training load that the individual athlete can consistently turn into improved performance without unacceptable injury, illness, or persistent fatigue.**

Sometimes that means increasing mileage.

Sometimes it means adding a second workout.

Sometimes it means turning a long run into a substantial marathon-specific session.

Sometimes it means repeating the same week.

And sometimes the best coaching decision is reducing workload—even when the athlete is motivated to do more.

The application should reproduce that judgment, not merely copy the workouts.

**Train hard enough to become exceptional. Recover well enough to keep doing it.**
